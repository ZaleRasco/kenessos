I want you to perform a deep, research-heavy analysis and redesign of my Kenessos, Priest of Thassa Commander deck. 

(commander is 1U for a 1/3 with oracle text: "If you would scry a number of cards, scry that many cards plus one instead. {3}{G/U}: Look at the top card of your library. If it's a Kraken, Leviathan, Octopus, or Serpent creature card, you may put it onto the battlefield. If you don't put the card onto the battlefield, you may put it on the bottom of your library.")

This is NOT primarily an exercise in maximizing win rate or making Kenessos as optimized and consistent as possible. I want to preserve and strengthen the deck's core fantasy while making it smoother, more varied, more interesting to replay, and more enjoyable for both me and the other players.

I am happy with substantial changes if they genuinely improve the deck, but I would prefer that you explore multiple levels of intervention rather than immediately replacing half the deck.

---

# 1. CORE POWER FANTASY

The central experience I love is:

**Manipulating the top of my library, leaving four mana available, and surprising the table by activating Kenessos at instant speed and suddenly producing an absurd Kraken, Leviathan, Octopus, or Serpent.**

That suspense and unpredictability is the heart of the deck.

I specifically enjoy:
- top-of-library manipulation
- knowing or partially knowing what Kenessos might hit
- activating Kenessos multiple times
- doing so at instant speed
- suddenly putting a ridiculous creature onto the battlefield for four mana
- giant sea monsters that feel dramatic, weird, or excessive

Do NOT optimize away this experience.

A redesign that mostly hard-casts creatures, uses unrelated cheat-into-play engines, or reduces Kenessos to incidental value would be a failure even if it were technically stronger.

---

# 2. TARGET POWER LEVEL

My target is:

**Mid-to-low power Bracket 3.**

I want the deck to:
- function reliably
- have real interaction
- do genuinely powerful things
- be capable of winning games
- feel meaningfully stronger than a casual precon-level deck

But I do NOT want:
- maximum-efficiency Bracket 3
- a deck tuned to compete with the strongest possible B3 lists
- every slot optimized for rate
- generic Simic goodstuff replacing personality
- high consistency at the cost of replayability

I am happy to spend some deck slots on thematic, strange, funny, or slightly inefficient cards if they create disproportionately good gameplay.

Use the CURRENT official Commander bracket definitions and CURRENT Game Changers list rather than relying on old information from memory.

Check the Game Changer status of my current deck and of any proposed versions.

Treat Game Changer slots as a limited design resource rather than assuming every powerful Game Changer should automatically stay.

---

# 3. PLAY ENVIRONMENT

I usually play:
- 4-player Commander
- mostly against Bracket 2–3 decks
- without one fixed, highly defined pod

This is primarily a **paper deck**, although I occasionally play digitally.

That matters because repeatedly shuffling a 100-card sleeved Commander deck is physically annoying in a way that digital shuffling is not.

Players have specifically complained about **Seedborn Muse** before.

Do not assume this automatically means Seedborn Muse must be removed. Analyze why it causes problems and whether the gameplay payoff is worth it.

---

# 4. WHAT I WANT TO IMPROVE

The current deck is fun, but I see several possible problems.

## A. It may be too one-dimensional

A lot of games can become:

ramp/setup  
→ manipulate topdeck  
→ activate Kenessos  
→ produce giant sea monsters  
→ attack with giant sea monsters

That is a good primary game plan. I do NOT want to remove it.

But I would like the deck to have more texture around that plan.

I am especially interested in finding interesting uses for enormous sea monsters OTHER than simply turning them sideways.

Explore possibilities such as:
- ETB or LTB exploitation
- bouncing and redeploying creatures
- copying creatures or abilities
- sacrificing creatures for meaningful value
- using enormous power/toughness as a resource
- unusual activated abilities
- creature-type interactions
- sea-monster tribal/typal payoffs
- topdeck interactions
- strange engines
- board-state manipulation
- alternate win conditions
- non-infinite synergistic engines
- late-game combos
- using sea monsters as resources rather than merely attackers

Do not force any of these ideas if they do not naturally fit.

I want you to DISCOVER which secondary mechanics actually integrate well with Kenessos.

---

## B. Too much searching/shuffling may make paper play cumbersome

I like topdeck manipulation.

Cards such as:
- Sensei's Divining Top
- Brainstorm
- Scroll Rack
- scry effects
- other ways to arrange or inspect the top of the deck

are fundamentally part of the fun.

The problem is NOT that I dislike manipulating the top of the library.

The problem is physically shuffling a large sleeved deck over and over.

Therefore distinguish carefully between:

**Topdeck manipulation**
which I enjoy,

and

**Library searching + reshuffling**
which I would prefer to reduce.

A few search-based ramp spells are probably inevitable and completely acceptable.

I am NOT asking for zero searches.

Prefer non-search alternatives when they accomplish roughly the same thing without significantly hurting the deck, but do not contort the mana base or ramp package merely to eliminate every shuffle.

Also analyze whether fetchlands are actually worth their repeated physical shuffling here given their synergy with cards such as Brainstorm, Scroll Rack, and Sensei's Divining Top.

---

## C. Too much opponent-turn gameplay may create a slog

Kenessos naturally encourages playing on opponents' turns.

That is part of the appeal.

I like having mana available and threatening:
- Kenessos activations
- interaction
- topdeck manipulation
- unexpected sea monsters

However, I want you to determine whether the deck currently crosses the line from:

**"I have exciting decisions on every turn"**

into:

**"Every opponent's turn partially becomes the Kenessos player's turn."**

Seedborn Muse is the clearest example.

The main reason I like Seedborn Muse is NOT simply generic mana advantage.

It is:

**more untaps = more mana = more Kenessos activations.**

That is extremely fun for me.

But it may also make rounds long or create repetitive gameplay, and other players have complained about it.

Analyze this tradeoff rather than automatically keeping or cutting Seedborn Muse.

Also consider the aggregate amount of opponent-turn activity created by cards such as:
- Kenessos itself
- Seedborn Muse
- instant-speed top manipulation
- counterspells
- Hullbreaker Horror
- Rewind-like effects
- other untap engines
- flash-speed interaction

A card might be fine individually but contribute to a package that collectively becomes tedious.

---

# 5. FUN / FRICTION FRAMEWORK

When evaluating cards, distinguish at least these four categories.

## A. Administrative friction

Examples:
- repeated library searching
- repeated shuffling
- excessive bookkeeping
- very slow topdeck manipulation
- repeated triggers
- long decision trees
- actions that make each round physically take much longer

## B. Repetitiveness

Cards or engines that make games converge toward the same sequence every time.

For example:
- always tutoring for the same card
- always assembling the same engine
- always producing the same lock
- always winning through the same route

## C. Opponent agency

Cards that make opponents feel unable to meaningfully execute their plans.

This can include:
- repeated bounce
- locks
- repeated countering
- resource denial
- board states where opponents technically take turns but cannot accomplish much

## D. My own enjoyment

Some cards may perform poorly under A–C while being extraordinarily fun for me.

Do NOT simply remove them.

Instead identify the tradeoff.

I want to consciously decide which "selfish fun" cards are worth keeping.

# 6. SPECIFIC CARDS / EFFECTS TO EVALUATE

## Seedborn Muse

Other players have complained about this card.

I love what it does because untapping repeatedly means many more Kenessos activations.

Please evaluate:
- how much it increases actual win rate
- how much it increases Kenessos fun
- how much it slows rounds
- how oppressive it actually is
- whether alternatives could preserve some of the "many activations" fantasy with less opponent-turn dominance
- whether it should remain in some versions of the deck but not others

Do not prejudge the answer.

---

## Temporal Mastery

I am uncertain about this card.

One extra turn may be annoying, OR the interaction between miracle and intentionally stacking the top of the deck may be interesting and thematically satisfying.

I have not played it enough to know.

Evaluate it based on:
- synergy
- novelty
- repetitive gameplay
- salt/opponent experience
- whether one occasional miracle extra turn is materially different from an extra-turn strategy

Do not treat "extra turn card = automatically unfun."

---

## Worldly Tutor

**Worldly Tutor should stay.**

It is one of the most fun cards in the deck because it can find ANY sea monster and put it on top for Kenessos.

This is exactly the kind of searching I am willing to tolerate because:
- it creates a highly thematic play
- it interacts directly with Kenessos
- the decision about WHICH monster to find can vary depending on the board
- it lets the deck behave like a toolbox

Do not cut it merely to reduce library searching.

Instead, consider whether Worldly Tutor suggests that a more interesting **sea-monster toolbox** identity is possible.

I am especially interested in whether the deck can become:

**a sea-monster toolbox/playground rather than merely a sea-monster slot machine.**

---

# 7. COMBO APPETITE

I do NOT want:
- random unrelated two-card "I win" combos
- compact deterministic combos that encourage me to tutor for the same two cards every game
- a deck whose actual hidden game plan becomes assembling a combo

I AM interested in a combo if it is:
- thematic
- clever
- multi-card
- naturally related to Kenessos or enormous creatures
- reachable with only a few sensible deck changes
- composed mostly or entirely of cards that are good and interesting individually
- likely to emerge organically in longer games

Ideally, removing any one combo piece should still leave me with cards I am happy to draw.

The combo does NOT have to be infinite.

A strange synergistic engine, overwhelming late-game interaction, recursive loop, or alternate route to victory may be even more interesting than a conventional infinite combo.

Because I do not want many tutors, a combo that only occasionally comes together late in a game could be ideal.

Search specifically to see whether such a package exists.

If there is no genuinely elegant combo, say so rather than forcing one.

# 9. BUDGET

Ignore budget.

Proxies are completely acceptable.

Do not reject a mechanically ideal card because of price.

However, price alone is not a reason to recommend something. Expensive staples should still justify their inclusion on gameplay grounds.

# 10. HOW RADICAL THE EDIT CAN BE

The edit may be radical if necessary.

However, I would prefer to see a **spectrum of possible revisions** rather than being handed one supposedly definitive replacement list.

Please develop approximately THREE versions:

## Option A — Conservative Refinement

Preserve most of the existing list.

Goals:
- reduce unnecessary shuffling
- remove obvious weak links
- improve smoothness
- reduce the worst repetitive/oppressive tendencies
- add a little more variety

This might involve roughly 5–12 meaningful swaps, though use judgment rather than following a fixed number.

## Option B — Recommended Redesign

Your best overall interpretation of my goals.

Make whatever changes are necessary to give the deck:
- stronger identity
- more varied gameplay
- one or more interesting secondary engines
- less unnecessary friction
- appropriate mid-to-low B3 power

This should probably be the version you think I would enjoy most.

## Option C — Weird Sea-Monster Laboratory

Push harder toward:
- obscure cards
- thematic synergies
- strange engines
- unusual uses for enormous creatures
- memorable board states
- interactions people probably have not seen before

It can sacrifice some raw efficiency, but it must still function as a legitimate mid-to-low Bracket 3 deck rather than becoming a pile of cute cards.

These versions should ideally share a recognizable common core.

Show me which packages or 10–20 cards distinguish the philosophies rather than creating three entirely unrelated decks.

# 14. IMPORTANT DESIGN PRINCIPLES

Do not confuse:

**smoothness**
with
**maximum consistency.**

A deck can be smooth because most of its cards are useful and its mana works without making every game unfold identically.

Likewise, do not confuse:

**interaction**
with
**oppression.**

I want meaningful interaction.

I do not necessarily want to dominate every other player's turn.

Do not fill every freed slot with:
- ramp
- tutors
- countermagic
- generic card draw
- generically powerful Simic staples

Do not reduce the sea-monster count so far that activating Kenessos stops feeling dangerous and exciting.

Do not remove quirky cards simply because something with a slightly better rate exists.

Prefer cards that increase the number of interesting decisions or possible game states.

---

# 15. THE CENTRAL QUESTION

The deepest design question I want you to investigate is:

**Can this deck become a sea-monster toolbox/playground rather than merely a sea-monster slot machine?**

I still want Kenessos activations to produce enormous surprise creatures.

But I would love for WHICH enormous creature appears to matter in more ways.

Can the monsters:
- solve different board states?
- interact with one another?
- form weird engines?
- become resources?
- create alternate lines?
- occasionally combine into something ridiculous?
- give Worldly Tutor interesting situational targets rather than one obviously best target?
- make different games unfold differently?

That is more valuable to me than simply increasing the deck's win percentage.
